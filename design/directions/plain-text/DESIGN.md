# Plain Text

Monospace as the body face, on purpose. High contrast, compact, sharp-cornered, and unadorned — it looks like notes written by someone who knows exactly what they're doing and didn't need to dress it up.

## Suits

Independent studios, architects and designers with a technical bent, specialty coffee roasters, repair and restoration workshops, makers, independent bookshops and record labels, developer-adjacent small businesses. Clients whose credibility is craft and candour.

## Avoid for

Mass-market consumer services, older audiences, anything that needs to feel warm, luxurious, or reassuring. Monospace body text is slower to read, so also avoid it for content-heavy sites with long pages.

## Posture

- **The typewriter is the personality.** Monospace everywhere; hierarchy comes from size and weight, not a second typeface.
- **Compact and exact.** Tight rhythm, short lines, nothing decorative.
- **Sharp corners, hairline rules.** Structure from borders and alignment, never from shadows.
- **Honest copy.** Plain statements of what's offered, what it costs, how long it takes.
- **High contrast.** Near-black on near-white, one accent for actions.

## Do

- Keep paragraphs very short — monospace punishes long blocks.
- Use lists and tables freely; they suit the voice.
- Use real photographs of the work, framed plainly.
- Let the page be spare. Empty space reads as deliberate here.

## Don't

- Don't write mock-technical copy ("> initializing services…"). The typeface is the nod; the words should be normal.
- No terminal-green-on-black cliché unless the client's brand genuinely is that.
- Don't use long body paragraphs. If a section needs 200 words, this is the wrong direction.

## Fonts

Space Mono at weight 700 for headings, DM Mono for body, when the client has none. Two different monospaces keep headings characterful and body text calm.
