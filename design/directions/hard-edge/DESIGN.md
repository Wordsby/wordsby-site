# Hard Edge

Loud and unapologetic. Heavy display type, square corners, thick borders, and solid offset shadows with no blur. Nothing is soft, nothing apologizes. It looks handmade in the way a screen-printed poster does.

## Suits

Creative agencies, bars and breweries, record shops, skate and streetwear, gyms and boxing clubs, festivals, anyone whose customers respond to confidence and personality. Works when the client is willing to be disliked by some people — that's the trade.

## Avoid for

Professional services, healthcare, finance, funeral homes, childcare — anywhere trust depends on calm. Also avoid it for clients with a lot of text: this direction is built for short, punchy copy, and long paragraphs in a heavy layout are exhausting.

## Posture

- **The headline is the design.** Set it big, keep it to a handful of words, and let it dominate.
- **Square everything.** No radius anywhere, on any element.
- **Borders are structural**, not decorative: three pixels, full strength, visible.
- **Shadows are solid offsets**, no blur. Objects look stacked on the page, not floating above it.
- **Copy is short and direct.** "Book a table." Not "We'd love to welcome you."

## Do

- Use the brand's boldest color at full strength, but still only twice per screen.
- Let sections butt directly against each other; the tone change is the separator.
- Keep body copy narrow. Wide measures fight the density of the type.

## Don't

- No soft shadows, no rounded anything, no delicate hairlines — mixing softness in makes it look like a mistake rather than a choice.
- Don't apply it to a client whose photography is gentle and pastel. The layout will bully the pictures.
- Don't let the heavy face run to three lines. If the headline is that long, cut the words, not the size.

## Fonts

Archivo Black for display, Space Grotesk for body, when the client has none. Archivo Black is a real poster weight rather than a bolded text face.
