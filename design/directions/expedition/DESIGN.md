# Expedition

The stencil voice of engineering and exploration: uppercase headings with positive tracking, a technical semi-condensed sans, and spare, capable layouts. It reads as equipment rather than decoration.

## Suits

Construction and civil engineering, security firms, drone and survey services, outdoor and expedition outfitters, marine and aviation services, heavy equipment, emergency and safety training. Clients whose audience values capability and reliability.

## Avoid for

Anything soft, personal, or intimate — therapists, weddings, childcare, beauty. Also avoid it for long reading: uppercase headings are fine, but the voice becomes tiring if every line shouts.

## Posture

- **Uppercase headings, tracked open.** Positive letter-spacing is what makes uppercase read as engineered rather than loud. Body text stays sentence case.
- **Technical and spare.** Semi-condensed type, straight edges with only slight softening, no shadows.
- **Photography carries scale.** Machinery, landscapes, sites, crews at work — wide shots that show size.
- **Facts in the foreground.** Coverage areas, fleet, certifications, response times, safety record.
- **One accent, used like a signal colour.**

## Do

- Keep headings short — uppercase slows reading, so fewer words matter more.
- Pair a wide, dramatic photograph with a short uppercase heading and one line of plain text.
- Use `feature-grid` for capabilities, with specific, quantified items.
- Use the `dark` tone for one band that shows scale, and keep the rest of the page light.

## Don't

- Don't uppercase body text, buttons with long labels, or anything longer than a heading.
- Don't reduce the tracking on uppercase to fit more in. Cut words instead.
- No military clichés, camouflage, or aggressive language. Capable, not combative.

## Fonts

Barlow Semi Condensed at weight 600 for headings, Barlow for body, when the client has none. One family in two widths: the condensed cut gives headings their technical voice, and the regular cut keeps body text easy to read.
