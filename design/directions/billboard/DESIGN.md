# Billboard

Giant display type with extreme negative tracking on a calm canvas. The headline is the hero image. Few words, enormous size, and a confident, crafted feel.

## Suits

Creative and branding studios, photographers and filmmakers, event and festival organisers, architects who want presence, product launches, agencies. Clients who can say what they do in four words and want to be remembered for it.

## Avoid for

Clients with a lot to explain, or a practical, information-seeking audience — trades, clinics, professional services. Also avoid it when headlines are long; giant type at six or seven words is a wall, not a statement.

## Posture

- **The headline is the design.** Set it enormous, tracked tightly, and give it the whole first screen if it earns it.
- **Few words, everywhere.** Section headings are short statements, not descriptions.
- **Calm around the noise.** Generous space, soft rounded shapes, no shadows — the type provides all the drama.
- **Crafted details.** Tight tracking, balanced line breaks, and consistent alignment matter more here than anywhere.
- **One accent for action.**

## Do

- Write headlines of three to five words.
- Use `text-wrap: balance` behaviour to your advantage — check every heading's line breaks at both widths.
- Pair a giant headline with one small, precise line of supporting text.
- Use large, strong imagery sparingly between type-led sections.

## Don't

- Don't shrink the display type to fit a long headline. Rewrite the headline.
- Don't combine giant type with busy imagery behind it.
- Don't let two giant headlines sit on the same screen. One statement at a time.

## Fonts

Schibsted Grotesk at weight 800 for headings, 400 for body, when the client has none. A grotesque that holds together at very tight tracking and very large sizes.
